I'm a computational physicist and modeler (PhD, Brandeis) with 5+ years of experience building numerical simulations of biological and soft-matter systems. My research spans a range of problems — cooperative cargo transport by teams of motor proteins, the emergent organization and motility of cell colonies, non-equilibrium transport (including TASEP-type models), and viral capsid self-assembly — tied together by a common goal: using physics-based modeling to uncover the mechanisms behind complex biological behavior.

That breadth is matched by a broad methodological toolkit. I developed implementations of GPU-accelerated coarse-grained molecular dynamics (HOOMD-blue), Monte Carlo and kinetic Monte Carlo, Gillespie stochastic kinetics, and active Brownian dynamics, running large simulation ensembles on HPC clusters with reproducible, modular Python analysis pipelines. I've also applied machine-learning methods to physics problems, and — from an earlier industry role — bring production data-pipeline and software-validation experience in a regulated (CDISC/clinical) setting.

My doctoral work centered on viral capsid assembly coupled to liquid–liquid phase separation, investigating how viruses exploit phase-separated compartments in host cells to replicate efficiently. This research, along with my earlier work, has appeared in journals including ACS Nano and Soft Matter.
Alongside the technical work, I value scientific communication, mentorship, and interdisciplinary collaboration, shaped in part by my time as a trainee at the Brandeis MRSEC. The common thread across the work I have done, is complex systems, and that's where I've found the work I want to keep doing — making sense of how simple rules give rise to emergent behavior, in biology, soft matter, and wherever the puzzle leads next
